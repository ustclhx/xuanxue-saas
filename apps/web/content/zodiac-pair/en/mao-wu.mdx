## How the two branches relate

Mao Wood generates Wu Fire, an arrow pointing from east to south. Each branch also stands at the very center of its three-harmony group: Mao is the peak of the Hai–Mao–Wei Wood group and Wu the peak of the Yin–Wu–Xu Fire group. So the pairing joins Wood at full strength with Fire at full strength, and the generating link makes the Wood side the supplier. Wu's group also contains the Dog as its storage stage; for that connection, see [Horse and Dog](/chinese-zodiac-compatibility/horse-and-dog).

## Pace and rhythm together

Between sunrise and noon the day goes from cool and soft to bright and hot. The Mao hour is when light first spills over the horizon and the household stirs. The Wu hour is the high point, when the sun stands overhead and energy is spent openly. Borrow these images and the relay is clear: the Rabbit side prepares and the Horse side carries the preparation to its peak.

Spring equinox to summer solstice tells the same story at a larger scale. Mao is the month of balance between day and night; Wu is the month when daylight is longest. Growth gathers in one and blazes in the other.

For friends running an event, colleagues on a launch, relatives organizing a gathering or partners at home, this suggests that one person gathers fuel and the other lights it up. The weak point is the middle of the morning, the stretch between the two hours. That gap is where the break image fits: preparation is finished, but the handoff has not happened, and momentum leaks. Name the moment of handover explicitly. Agree on what ready looks like, then let the faster side run.

## What each can learn from the other

Fuel without a flame stays a pile of sticks; flame without fuel burns out. Each side carries a reminder for the other. Questions worth sitting with:

- When I prepare, do I ever release the work to be used, or do I keep refining?
- When I am in full motion, do I notice who supplied what I am burning through?
- Where do I need more warmth, and where more patience?

A small exercise: list everything the two of you rely on each other for, even trivially. Mark each item with an arrow showing which way it flows. If most arrows point the same direction, discuss one way to send something back. Repeat the list a season later and see whether the flow has shifted, or whether the same person still carries the kindling.

## Where friction tends to show up

The generating arrow can tilt into imbalance: one side keeps supplying while the other keeps spending. The break adds a second theme, interruption. Arrangements falter not with a bang but with a dropped thread. Neither of these is an outcome; they are tendencies worth planning around.

Concrete steps help:

1. Take turns being the one who prepares, so the supplier role rotates.
2. When a plan stalls halfway, ask which piece fell between the two of you, rather than who dropped it.
3. Schedule a regular midpoint check, a short look at how things stand before anyone is tired.
4. Keep a short record of commitments so threads do not quietly disappear.

## Reading two full BaZi charts

Suppose one person was born in 1999 (Ji Mao) and the other in 2002 (Ren Wu). The year branches carry the Mao–Wu break and a Wood-to-Fire arrow. The stems point elsewhere: Ji is yin Earth and Ren is yang Water, and Earth controls Water. The two year pillars show both a generating link between the branches and a controlling link between the stems.

The year branch remains one of eight characters per chart. A Wei in either chart's month pillar is notable here, because it forms the Wu–Wei six harmony with the Horse and also shares the Wood group with the Rabbit's Mao. A Zi in a day pillar would bring the Zi–Wu clash; a You would bring the Mao–You clash. Build both charts from birth date and time, then compare each pillar before settling on what the year relation adds.
