## How to use this spread

Bring one specific question, such as a role you are weighing, a project that has stalled, or a conversation with a manager you keep putting off. The cards are a way to look at it from four sides. They are not career, legal or financial advice, and they work best alongside the practical information you already have.

Read the four cards in numbered order. The first pair gives you two things to consider together: what is happening and what resources you bring to it. The second pair connects the obstacle with a possible response. If the first card makes you think of a tight deadline, for example, the second might prompt you to recall a way you handled a similar workload.

Note the date and your question before you start. Work questions tend to come back months later in a slightly different form, and an old spread is a good record of how you were thinking at the time. To look up a card in more depth, see the [tarot meanings](/tarot/cards) library.

## What the suits suggest in a work question

The 56 cards of the Minor Arcana fall into four suits, and in a work reading the suit of a card is often the quickest clue to its theme. Pentacles are traditionally tied to earth: money, materials, steady effort and craft. The Eight of Pentacles, with the keywords practice and diligence, depicts a craftsperson engraving one pentacle with others already on display.

In the same tradition, Swords correspond to air and suggest themes such as planning, arguing and deciding. The Two of Swords (stalemate, deliberation) shows a blindfolded figure holding a sword in each hand. The image can prompt you to ask what would help you weigh a choice you have been putting off. Wands correspond to fire, which readers associate with initiative and ambition. The Ace of Wands stands for inspiration and initiative. Cups, the water suit, turn the question toward people, morale and how the work feels.

The 22 cards of the Major Arcana sit outside the suits. If several appear in a four-card spread, you can use them to ask a broader question about your work. For example, what would you like to learn in this role, and which parts of the job give you a chance to practice it?

## Turning the last card into a step

To read the fourth card, start with its keywords and pick one action small enough to try this week. The Page of Wands (curiosity, exploration) might prompt you to invite someone from another team for coffee. The Seven of Pentacles (patience, assessment) might prompt you to list the information you still need and check when it will be available.

Then hold the step up against the first card. If the situation is crowded and urgent, a slow step may not fit. If the situation is calm, a bold step may be easier than it looks. The third card is a useful check too: a step that walks straight into the named obstacle is worth reconsidering.

## When the spread feels stuck

Sometimes all four cards bring the same concern to mind. If that is all you can see in the spread, write the concern down in one sentence. You can also narrow a broad question to one decision or one working relationship and come back to it another day.

A heavy card in the third position, such as the Ten of Wands (burden, responsibility), can prompt you to list the work you are carrying. Read it alongside the second card and ask where your own experience is most useful, or whether there is a task you could share with a colleague.

For a question that involves many people and moving parts, the ten-card [Celtic Cross spread](/tarot/celtic-cross) gives each part its own position. For a quick look at how the situation got here, a [three-card tarot](/tarot) reading sets past, present and future side by side.
