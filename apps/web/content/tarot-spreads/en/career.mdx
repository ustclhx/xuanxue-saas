## How to use this spread

Bring one specific question, such as a role you are weighing, a project that has stalled, or a conversation with a manager you keep putting off. The cards are a way to look at it from four sides. They are not career, legal or financial advice, and they work best alongside the practical information you already have.

Lay the cards out in a row from left to right. The first two describe your starting point: the situation itself and the resources you carry into it. The last two look at movement: what slows you down and what you might do about it. Reading them as two pairs makes it easier to see whether the spread is pointing at the situation or at you.

Note the date and your question before you start. Work questions tend to come back months later in a slightly different form, and an old spread is a good record of how you were thinking at the time. To look up a card in more depth, see the [tarot meanings](/tarot/cards) library.

## What the suits suggest in a work question

The 56 cards of the Minor Arcana fall into four suits, and in a work reading the suit of a card is often the quickest clue to its theme. Pentacles are traditionally tied to earth: money, materials, steady effort and craft. The Eight of Pentacles, with the keywords practice and diligence, is a familiar figure in career spreads, an apprentice working through one piece after another.

Swords belong to air and to the mind: planning, arguing, deciding. The Two of Swords (stalemate, deliberation) shows a blindfolded figure holding two blades in balance, a picture of a choice that has been postponed. Wands carry fire, which readers associate with initiative and ambition; the Ace of Wands stands for inspiration and initiative. Cups, the water suit, turn the question toward people, morale and how the work feels.

The 22 cards of the Major Arcana sit outside the suits. When several appear in a four-card spread, many readers take it as a sign that the question touches something larger than the job description, such as how you want to spend your working years.

## Turning the last card into a step

The fourth card is the reason this spread exists, so give it the most time. Start with its keywords, then ask what the smallest version of that theme would look like in your week. The Page of Wands (curiosity, exploration) might become a coffee with someone in another team. The Seven of Pentacles (patience, assessment) might mean waiting another month before deciding, and writing down what you would need to see in that time.

Then hold the step up against the first card. If the situation is crowded and urgent, a slow step may not fit. If the situation is calm, a bold step may be easier than it looks. The third card is a useful check too: a step that walks straight into the named obstacle is worth reconsidering.

## When the spread feels stuck

Sometimes all four cards seem to say the same thing, or nothing at all. That usually means the question was too wide. Narrow it to one decision or one relationship and draw again on another day.

A heavy card in the third position, such as the Ten of Wands (burden, responsibility), is not a verdict on your career. It describes weight you are already carrying. Read with the second card, it can suggest which part of that load you could hand on.

For a question that involves many people and moving parts, the ten-card [Celtic Cross spread](/tarot/celtic-cross) gives each part its own position. For a quick look at how the situation got here, a [three-card tarot](/tarot) reading sets past, present and future side by side.
