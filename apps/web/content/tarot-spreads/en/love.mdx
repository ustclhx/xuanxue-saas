## How to use this spread

The spread works for a partner, someone you are getting to know, a friendship that matters to you, or a relationship that has already ended. It does not tell you what someone else feels or whether a relationship will last. What it offers is a structured way to look at your own side of the picture, one position at a time.

Before you shuffle, write your question in a single line, or hold it clearly in mind. Questions that start with "what" or "how" give you room to consider several possible answers. Then shuffle, cut the deck and turn the cards over in order, from the first position to the fifth.

Give each card a minute before moving on. Read the keywords, then ask how they fit the position's label. The meanings on this page are short summaries from the Rider–Waite–Smith tradition; for a fuller entry on any card, open the [tarot meanings](/tarot/cards) library. For a shorter draw, a [three-card tarot](/tarot) reading covers past, present and future.

## Reading the cards together

Once all five cards are face up, compare them in pairs. The first and second positions sit side by side on purpose: one describes you, the other describes the picture you hold of the other person. If the two cards share a suit or a mood, ask whether you are seeing your own state reflected in them. If they look very different, ask what you might be missing about either side.

The third and fourth positions work as a pair as well. Think about what you value in the relationship and where you notice tension. Sometimes the same card theme appears in both. The Two of Cups, for example, carries the keywords connection and reciprocity, while its reversed form points to imbalance. If it appears upright in the third position and the Five of Wands (competition, friction) appears in the fourth, think of a time you worked well together and a time a small disagreement turned into a contest. What was different about how you spoke to each other?

The fifth card is the one to take away. Rather than reading it as an outcome, phrase it as something you could ask. The Star, whose keywords are hope and renewal, might become: "What would I say if I trusted it would be heard?"

## Questions that work well

The spread works well with questions about your own part in a relationship. "What am I avoiding saying?" and "What do I need from this friendship?" give each position something to work with. "What would help us talk more openly?" can help you name something you could bring up together.

The cards cannot answer questions about someone else's private feelings or actions. If that is what brought you here, try reframing the question around your own experience. What have you noticed in your interactions? What would you want to ask them directly?

If a relationship question has many sides, such as a family conflict that also involves money and work, the ten-card [Celtic Cross spread](/tarot/celtic-cross) gives each part its own position.

## When a card feels uncomfortable

Some cards are hard to look at in a love reading. The Three of Swords shows a heart pierced by three blades, and its keywords are sorrow and painful truth. The Tower stands for disruption and revelation. These images can bring up feelings about events you already know about. Try writing down one specific memory the card brings to mind.

Reversed cards deserve the same patience. Some readers interpret a reversed card as a theme turned inward, blocked or overdone. The Lovers upright speak of alignment and choice, and the reversed card points to discord or misalignment. You might ask whether you have agreed on what you each want or whether there is still a conversation to have.

If a spread leaves you uneasy, write down the card that bothers you most and what it brought to mind. That note is often more useful than the cards themselves, and you can come back to it after a conversation has happened.
