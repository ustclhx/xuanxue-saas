## What a birth chart shows

A birth chart, or natal chart, maps planetary positions onto the zodiac at the time of your birth. It also marks the Ascendant and Midheaven, two points calculated for the place you were born. The wheel gives you a view of the whole chart, and the tables below it give the positions in degrees and minutes.

Most people start with three points: the Sun, the Moon and the rising sign. If those are all you need, the [big three](/rising-sign-calculator) page finds them in one step. The full chart adds Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto, the mean North Node, the Midheaven, the twelve houses and the aspects between the planets. Each planet sits in one of the twelve [Western zodiac signs](/zodiac-signs), at a degree from 0 to 29 within that sign.

The chart does not include Chiron, Lilith, asteroids or the Part of Fortune. It sticks to the ten planets, the node and the two main angles, which are the points shown in the wheel and the planet table.

## How to read the chart wheel

The outer ring is the zodiac, divided into twelve signs of 30° each. The wheel puts the Ascendant on the left and labels the Midheaven MC. The numbered sectors are the houses. A cusp marks the start of each house, and the house numbers run counterclockwise.

Four points frame the wheel. The Ascendant is where the ecliptic met the eastern horizon, and the Descendant is the point directly opposite it. The Midheaven is the point on the ecliptic on the local meridian opposite the IC, or lower culmination. In Placidus, the Ascendant, Descendant, Midheaven and IC mark the first, seventh, tenth and fourth cusps, respectively. Whole-sign cusps fall at the start of each sign. Dots mark the planets’ exact degrees, with leader lines where their symbols are crowded. The lines across the center show up to ten of the closest aspects.

A simple way into a chart is to find the Sun, the Moon and the Ascendant first, then notice which houses hold several planets and which hold none. With ten planets and twelve houses, at least two houses always have no planets. Astrologers can still look at the signs on those house cusps.

## Planets, degrees and retrograde motion

The planet table lists each body with its sign, its degree and minute within the sign, the house it falls in and whether it was moving direct or retrograde. A planet is marked retrograde if it appeared to move backward through the zodiac as viewed from Earth. The label describes a change in apparent motion due to the relative motion of Earth and the planet.

How often a planet appears retrograde depends on the planet, and the [retrograde calendar](/retrograde-calendar) lists the dates for this year and next. Calculated day by day from 1900 through 2026, Mercury was retrograde on about 19% of days, Venus on about 7% and Mars on about 9%, while Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto were each retrograde on between about 30% and 44% of days. The Sun and Moon are never retrograde. The mean North Node, a calculated point rather than a body, always moves backward through the zodiac.

Mercury stays within about 28° of the Sun, and Venus within about 48°. That puts Mercury in the Sun’s sign or a neighboring sign, and Venus within two signs of the Sun. Tradition reads each planet as a different part of life: astrologers associate Mercury with thinking and speech, Venus with affection and taste, Mars with drive, and Saturn with structure and time. The sign colors how that part is described, and the house shows the area of life where tradition places it.

## Houses and the house system

The twelve houses divide the zodiac using the time and place of your birth. There are several ways to draw them. This calculator uses Placidus houses by default, a time-based system named after the 17th-century astrologer Placidus de Titis and widely used in modern Western astrology. Whole-sign houses are offered as an alternative: each house is simply one whole sign, starting with the rising sign. In that system the Ascendant can fall anywhere within the first house.

The two systems often place a planet in different houses, especially when it sits near a cusp, so it helps to know which one you are looking at. Next to the planet table, the house cusp list gives the start of each house in the system shown on the chart.

At high latitudes the Placidus method breaks down: for part of the day some cusps can't be calculated at all. Beyond 66° north or south, the calculator switches automatically to whole-sign houses and shows a notice above the wheel.

## Aspects and orbs

An aspect is an angle between two planets along the zodiac. The chart looks for five major aspects: the conjunction at 0°, the sextile at 60°, the square at 90°, the trine at 120° and the opposition at 180°. Planets rarely form an exact angle, so each aspect has an orb, the allowed distance from exact: 8° for the conjunction, square, trine and opposition, and 6° for the sextile. Aspects are calculated between the ten planets only, which gives 45 possible pairs, and the table lists them from the closest to the widest.

Aspects use a finer measurement than comparing signs. A sign-level comparison treats two whole signs as a pair, while an aspect looks at the exact degrees, so two planets in neighboring signs can still form a conjunction, and two planets in signs that are four apart can fall outside a trine's orb.

Some aspects are shared by large groups of people. Neptune and Pluto move so slowly that, in a sample taken every ten days from 1941 through 2026, they fell within the 6° orb of a sextile more often than they fell outside it. Many astrologers give more personal weight to aspects involving the Sun, Moon and faster planets, which change from week to week or day to day. Tradition reads the conjunction as a blending of two planets, the trine and sextile as ease, and the square and opposition as tension that asks for adjustment.

## How the chart is calculated

The wheel measures apparent positions from Earth’s center along the ecliptic. It uses the tropical zodiac, whose Aries point is fixed to the March equinox. Planetary positions are calculated for a single instant, and the axes and houses also use the coordinates of your chosen birthplace. The calculator accepts dates from 1900 through today. You can draw the chart in your browser without creating an account.

We verified the calculations against 30 Swiss Ephemeris charts. Checks included charts near the date line and beyond 66° latitude, dates before 1970 and local times around clock changes. In that test set, each planetary position was within one arcminute of the reference. The Ascendant and Midheaven were within 0.05°, the Placidus cusps were within 0.1°, and all signs and house assignments matched.

If you don’t know your birth time, the calculator uses local noon to find the Sun and Moon signs and checks whether either changed signs that day. When a sign changes, the result gives both possibilities and the approximate local time of the change. The wheel, houses and angles require a birth time.

Positions are calculated with standard astronomical methods. Sign, house and aspect meanings come from traditional astrology and are for reference and self-reflection only. The chart does not predict the future.
