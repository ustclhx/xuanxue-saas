## What a birth chart shows

A birth chart, or natal chart, is a map of the sky for one moment, seen from one place. It records where the Sun, the Moon and the planets stood in the zodiac when you were born, which part of the zodiac was rising in the east, and which part was highest in the sky. This calculator draws that map as a wheel and lists the same positions in tables underneath, so you can read each value exactly.

Most people start with three points: the Sun, the Moon and the rising sign. If those are all you need, the [big three](/rising-sign-calculator) page finds them in one step. The full chart adds Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto, the mean North Node, the Midheaven, the twelve houses and the aspects between the planets. Each planet sits in one of the twelve [Western zodiac signs](/zodiac-signs), at a degree from 0 to 29 within that sign.

The chart does not include Chiron, Lilith, asteroids or the Part of Fortune. It sticks to the ten planets, the node and the two main angles, which are the points almost every astrologer reads first.

## How to read the chart wheel

The outer ring is the zodiac, divided into twelve signs of 30° each. The Ascendant, the degree that was rising on the eastern horizon, is placed on the left, and the Midheaven sits near the top. The spokes inside the zodiac ring are the house cusps, and the houses are numbered counter-clockwise from the Ascendant, so the first house lies just below the left-hand horizon and the tenth house begins at the Midheaven.

Four points frame the wheel. The Ascendant is where the ecliptic met the eastern horizon, and the Descendant is the point directly opposite it. The Midheaven is where the ecliptic crossed your local meridian, and the point opposite it is often called the IC. In the Placidus system these four begin the first, seventh, tenth and fourth houses. Each planet glyph is placed at its own degree, and the lines in the centre join pairs of planets that form one of the major aspects.

A simple way into a chart is to find the Sun, the Moon and the Ascendant first, then notice which houses hold several planets and which hold none. An empty house is common and is not read as a missing part of life; with ten planets and twelve houses, at least two houses are always empty.

## Planets, degrees and retrograde motion

The planet table lists each body with its sign, its degree and minute within the sign, the house it falls in and whether it was moving direct or retrograde. A planet is marked retrograde when, seen from the Earth, it appeared to move backward through the zodiac at that moment. The effect is apparent rather than real: it comes from the Earth and the planet moving around the Sun at different speeds.

How often a planet appears retrograde depends on the planet. Calculated day by day from 1900 through 2026, Mercury was retrograde on about 19% of days, Venus on about 7% and Mars on about 9%, while Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto were each retrograde on between about 30% and 44% of days. The Sun and Moon are never retrograde. The mean North Node, a calculated point rather than a body, always moves backward through the zodiac.

Some positions are tied to the Sun. Over the same period Mercury was never more than about 28° from the Sun, and Venus never more than about 47°, so Mercury is always in the Sun's sign or a neighbouring one, and Venus is never more than two signs away. Tradition reads each planet as a different part of life: astrologers associate Mercury with thinking and speech, Venus with affection and taste, Mars with drive, and Saturn with structure and time. The sign colours how that part is described, and the house shows the area of life where tradition places it.

## Houses and the house system

The twelve houses divide the sky around your birthplace, starting from the Ascendant. There are several ways to draw them. This calculator uses Placidus houses by default, a time-based system named after the 17th-century astrologer Placidus de Titis and widely used in modern Western astrology. Whole-sign houses are offered as an alternative: each house is simply one whole sign, starting with the rising sign. Whole-sign houses are thought to be the oldest method and were the main system in Hellenistic astrology.

The two systems often place a planet in different houses, especially when it sits near a cusp, so it helps to know which one you are looking at. The house cusp list under the planet table gives the degree where each house begins in the system you chose.

At high latitudes the Placidus method breaks down: for part of the day some cusps can't be calculated at all. Above 66° latitude, north or south, the chart therefore switches to whole-sign houses automatically and says so above the wheel, rather than showing a chart with missing cusps.

## Aspects and orbs

An aspect is an angle between two planets along the zodiac. The chart looks for five major aspects: the conjunction at 0°, the sextile at 60°, the square at 90°, the trine at 120° and the opposition at 180°. Planets rarely form an exact angle, so each aspect has an orb, the allowed distance from exact: 8° for the conjunction, square, trine and opposition, and 6° for the sextile. Aspects are calculated between the ten planets only, which gives 45 possible pairs, and the table lists them from the closest to the widest.

This is more precise than comparing signs. A sign-level comparison treats two whole signs as a pair, while an aspect looks at the exact degrees, so two planets in neighbouring signs can still form a conjunction, and two planets in signs that are four apart can fall outside a trine's orb.

Some aspects are shared by large groups of people. Neptune and Pluto move so slowly that, calculated day by day from 1900 through 2026, they were within the 6° orb of a sextile on most days since 1941. Many astrologers give more personal weight to aspects involving the Sun, Moon and faster planets, which change from week to week or day to day. Tradition reads the conjunction as a blending of two planets, the trine and sextile as ease, and the square and opposition as tension that asks for adjustment.

## How the chart is calculated

Your local birth time is converted to universal time using the time zone of the place you chose, including the daylight saving time and the historic UTC offset that applied on that date, from the IANA time-zone database. Places come from GeoNames data for towns and cities with a population of 15,000 or more; if your birthplace is smaller, choose the nearest larger town. Positions are apparent geocentric longitudes in the tropical zodiac, and the free chart is calculated in your browser, with no sign-up needed. Dates from 1900 through today are accepted.

Before release, the calculation was compared with Swiss Ephemeris reference charts in 30 sample births, covering both hemispheres, high latitudes, days when the clocks changed, births before 1970 and places near the date line. Planet positions agreed to within one arc-minute, the Ascendant and Midheaven to within 0.05°, Placidus cusps to within 0.1°, and every sign and house matched.

If you don't know your birth time, the planets are shown by sign for noon local time on your birth date. If the Moon changed sign that day, both possible signs are listed with the local time of the change. The Ascendant, Midheaven, houses and wheel are left out, because they depend on the hour.

Positions are calculated with standard astronomical methods. Sign, house and aspect meanings come from traditional astrology and are for reference and self-reflection only; the chart does not predict the future.
