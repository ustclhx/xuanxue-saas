## Where the name comes from

The Worm Moon is March's entry in the list popularized by the Maine Farmers' Almanac in the 1930s and repeated by The Old Farmer's Almanac ever since. Like its neighbors, the name was gathered from a mix of Native American, Colonial American and European folk usage rather than invented for a single calendar.

Almanacs most often explain it with earthworms. As the ground softens, worm casts start to show on lawns and bare soil, one of the first small signs that winter is loosening. Some writers point instead to an eighteenth-century account by the explorer Jonathan Carver, which connects the name with beetle larvae working their way out of thawing bark. The two stories agree on the season even if they differ on the creature.

Other March names describe the same transition. The Sap Moon recalls the weeks when people tapped maple trees and boiled sap into syrup, and the Crow Moon is often linked to the loud return of crows. ## Viewing tips

March is a month of balance in the sky. Near the equinox the full moon rises close to due east around sunset and sets close to due west around sunrise, following a path that is neither as high as in winter nor as low as in summer. For a photo, find a street or field edge that faces east and wait for the Moon to clear the horizon.

The evening sky after new moon is especially rewarding now. Spring is when the Moon's path meets the western horizon most steeply after sunset, so a crescent only a day or two old can stand clear of the twilight glow. Watch it thicken each evening through the following week, and look for earthshine softening the dark side of the disk.

The March full moon also matters on the calendar. Many Christian churches set the date of Easter from the first full moon on or after the spring equinox, using a fixed ecclesiastical reckoning rather than the exact astronomical moment. That is why some years' Easter dates line up with the March full moon and others with April's. Our [full moon dates](/full-moon-calendar) table shows how this year's moons fall.

Daylight saving time begins in March across much of North America and Europe, which shifts moonrise and moonset times by an hour on your clock. The Moon has not changed, only the clock has. If a time looks off by an hour, compare it with [moon phase today](/moon-phase-today), which uses your device's current time zone. South of the equator, March brings the autumn equinox instead.
