## Where the name comes from

The Old Farmer’s Almanac calls March’s full moon the Worm Moon. An earlier record appears in Carver’s account of his travels in the 1760s. He describes a lunar month associated with spring, with its start tied to the new moon, whereas the modern almanac uses the label for a full moon in Gregorian March.

Almanacs most often explain it with earthworms. As the ground softens, worm casts start to show on lawns and bare soil, one of the first small signs that winter is loosening. Carver recorded a different image: worms leaving the tree bark and wood where they had spent the winter. Modern almanacs have suggested he may have meant beetle larvae. Both accounts link the name to the arrival of spring.

Other March names describe the same transition. The Sap Moon recalls the weeks when people tapped maple trees and boiled sap into syrup, and the Crow Comes Back Moon marks the return of crows. To find the date of the next full moon, check [full moon tonight](/full-moon).

## Viewing tips

March is a month of balance in the sky. Near the equinox the full moon rises close to due east around sunset and sets close to due west around sunrise, following a path that is neither as high as in winter nor as low as in summer. For a photo, find a street or field edge that faces east and wait for the Moon to clear the horizon.

The evening sky after new moon is especially rewarding now. Spring is when the Moon's path meets the western horizon most steeply after sunset, so a crescent only a day or two old can stand clear of the twilight glow. Watch it thicken each evening through the following week, and look for earthshine softening the dark side of the disk.

The March full moon also matters on the calendar. In the Western Christian calendar, Easter falls on the Sunday after the first ecclesiastical full moon on or after March 21. Both the equinox date and the ecclesiastical full moon follow calendar rules, so the latter can fall on a different date from the astronomical full moon. Our [full moon dates](/full-moon-calendar) table shows how this year's moons fall.

Daylight saving time begins in March across much of North America and Europe, which shifts moonrise and moonset times by an hour on your clock. The Moon has not changed, only the clock has. The [moon phase today](/moon-phase-today) page shows phase times in your device’s time zone. For moonrise and moonset, use a table for your location. South of the equator, March brings the autumn equinox instead.
