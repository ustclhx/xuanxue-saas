## Where the name comes from

The Beaver Moon is part of the familiar set of month names spread by North American almanacs, notably the Maine Farmers' Almanac in the 1930s and The Old Farmer's Almanac. Those almanacs drew on a mix of Native American, Colonial American and European folk names, and the November entry is one of the most widely repeated.

Almanacs explain it through the beaver's late-autumn routine. Before ponds and streams ice over, beavers repair their lodges with mud and sticks and sink a supply of branches near the entrance to feed on through winter. That activity is easy to spot along wetlands in November, when trees have dropped their leaves. Fresh-cut stumps with pointed, gnawed tips are a common sign that a beaver family is working nearby.

A second strand of the story comes from the fur trade. Historically, trappers set their lines in November, while the beavers' coats were at their thickest and before the swamps froze solid. Another widely listed name, the Frost Moon, keeps the focus on the weather, marking the month when hard frosts settle in across northern regions.

## Viewing tips

November is a generous month for moon watching. Darkness falls early, so a rising full moon is visible soon after dinner rather than late at night. Because the Sun is low at midday, the full moon opposite it climbs well up into the southern sky for mid-northern observers and stays above the horizon for a long stretch of the night.

The lunar disk often sits among the stars of Taurus and Aries at this time of year. On a clear night, look for the Pleiades, a tight cluster of blue-white stars, somewhere nearby; binoculars help pick them out against the moonlight. Cold, dry air can make the view especially crisp, so dress for a long stay outside. A folding chair, a warm drink and a red flashlight that protects your night vision make the evening far more comfortable.
