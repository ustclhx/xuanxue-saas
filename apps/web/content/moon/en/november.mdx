## Where the name comes from

Beaver Moon appears in Jonathan Carver’s eighteenth-century account of Indigenous lunar months. It is the November name used by The Old Farmer’s Almanac, which gives two explanations linked to the work of preparing for winter.

Almanacs explain it through the beaver's late-autumn routine. Before ponds and streams ice over, beavers repair their lodges with mud and sticks and sink a supply of branches near the entrance to feed on through winter. That activity is easy to spot along wetlands in November, when trees have dropped their leaves. Fresh-cut stumps with pointed, gnawed tips are a common sign that a beaver family is working nearby.

The Old Farmer’s Almanac also connects the name to trapping during the North American fur trade, when beavers had grown thick winter coats. Frost Moon, which the same almanac credits to Cree and Assiniboine peoples, describes a change in the weather.

## Viewing tips

In November, you can often see the full moon soon after sunset. Darkness falls early, so a rising full moon is visible soon after dinner rather than late at night. Because the Sun is low at midday, the full moon opposite it climbs well up into the southern sky for mid-northern observers and stays above the horizon for a long stretch of the night.

The lunar disk often sits among the stars of Taurus and Aries at this time of year. On a clear night, look for the Pleiades, a tight cluster of blue-white stars, somewhere nearby; binoculars help pick them out against the moonlight. Cold, dry air can make the view especially crisp, so dress for a long stay outside. A folding chair, a warm drink and a dim red flashlight for reading a chart make the evening far more comfortable.
