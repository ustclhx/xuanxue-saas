## What counts as a full moon

Astronomers define the full moon as a precise moment: the instant when the Moon’s ecliptic longitude is 180 degrees ahead of the Sun’s, as measured from Earth’s center. Outside a lunar eclipse, almost all of the visible disk is lit by sunlight around that time. The exact time can fall at any hour of the day or night, which is why this page gives both the date and the minute.

Because the Moon's orbit is tilted about five degrees to Earth's orbit, the Moon usually passes a little above or below Earth's shadow at full moon. A lunar eclipse occurs when the full moon passes through Earth’s shadow. That takes a full moon near one of the two points where its orbit crosses the plane of Earth’s orbit.

To the eye, the Moon looks full for roughly a night before and a night after the exact instant. If the precise moment falls in the afternoon where you live, both that evening and the previous one show a nearly perfect disk. For the full list of upcoming dates in your time zone, see the [full moon dates](/full-moon-calendar) page.

The cycle from one full moon to the next, the synodic month, averages about 29.5 days. That is why most calendar months contain exactly one full moon and why the date usually moves earlier across successive months.

## Supermoons and blue moons

Supermoon is an informal name for a full moon near perigee, the Moon’s closest approach to Earth in a given orbit. The term was coined in 1979 in an astrological context, and definitions vary. This page marks a full moon as a supermoon when it occurs within 24 hours before or after perigee.

A supermoon looks somewhat larger and brighter than a full moon near apogee, the farthest point, but the difference is hard to notice without a side-by-side photo. Viewing conditions such as haze can make the difference even harder to see.

The phrase blue moon has two common meanings. The monthly definition, used on this page, refers to the second full moon in the same calendar month in your time zone. Because the synodic month is a little shorter than most calendar months, two full moons can fit into a long month when the first occurs early enough. In seasonal usage, a blue moon is the third of four full moons in one astronomical season. The Maine Farmers’ Almanac used this idea with its own convention for setting season boundaries. Neither kind has anything to do with the Moon's color.

## Watching a full moon rise

The full moon sits roughly opposite the Sun, so it rises in the east around sunset and sets in the west around sunrise. Near the horizon, the disk can look orange or amber. Moonlight travels through more air there, and the atmosphere scatters more of the blue light out of its path.

Choose a spot with a clear, low eastern horizon, such as a beach, a hilltop or an open field. Look up the moonrise time for your location and arrive early. Phase time tells you when the Moon reaches full. Moonrise tells you when it crosses the local horizon, and hills or buildings can delay your first view. A pair of binoculars shows the rim of the disk wobbling in the shimmering air near the horizon.

Season changes the view. For mid-northern latitudes, a winter full moon climbs high overhead and stays up for many hours, while a summer full moon traces a low arc across the southern sky. Near the northern autumn equinox, moonrise can come only a little later on consecutive evenings around full moon. This is the familiar viewing effect associated with the Harvest Moon, the full moon closest to the September equinox.

If you miss the exact night, the Moon will still look full the evening after. You can follow how the shape changes from night to night on [today's moon phase](/moon-phase-today) page, or plan further ahead with the [moon phase calendar](/moon-calendar).
