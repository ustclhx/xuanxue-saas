## What counts as a full moon

Astronomers define the full moon as a precise moment: the instant when the Moon and Sun are on opposite sides of Earth, separated by 180 degrees in ecliptic longitude. At that moment the entire face turned toward us is lit by sunlight. The exact time can fall at any hour of the day or night, which is why this page gives both the date and the minute.

Because the Moon's orbit is tilted about five degrees to Earth's orbit, the Moon usually passes a little above or below Earth's shadow at full moon. Only when the alignment is very close does a lunar eclipse occur, which is why eclipses happen just a few times a year rather than every month.

To the eye, the Moon looks full for roughly a night before and a night after the exact instant. If the precise moment falls in the afternoon where you live, both that evening and the previous one show a nearly perfect disk. For the full list of upcoming dates in your time zone, see the [full moon dates](/full-moon-calendar) page.

The cycle from one full moon to the next, the synodic month, averages about 29.5 days. That is why most calendar months contain exactly one full moon and the date drifts earlier by a day or two each month.

## Supermoons and blue moons

A supermoon is a full moon that occurs when the Moon is near perigee, the closest point in its elliptical orbit. The term came from popular astrology rather than astronomy, and there is no single agreed definition. Some sources use a distance threshold, others a percentage of the closest possible approach. This page takes a simple, conservative approach and marks a full moon as a supermoon when it falls within about a day of perigee.

A supermoon looks somewhat larger and brighter than a full moon near apogee, the farthest point, but the difference is hard to notice without a side-by-side photo. It is a pleasant reason to step outside rather than a dramatic change in the sky.

The phrase blue moon has two common meanings. The monthly definition, used on this page, refers to the second full moon in a single calendar month. Because the synodic month is a little shorter than most calendar months, this happens now and then when a full moon falls in the first day or two of a month. An older seasonal definition, kept by some almanacs, calls a blue moon the third full moon in an astronomical season that contains four. Neither kind has anything to do with the Moon's color.

## Watching a full moon rise

The full moon sits roughly opposite the Sun, so it rises in the east around sunset and sets in the west around sunrise. Moonrise is one of the most rewarding moments to watch: the disk appears on the horizon with a warm orange or amber tint, because its light passes through a long column of atmosphere that scatters away the blue.

Choose a spot with a clear, low eastern horizon, such as a beach, a hilltop or an open field. Check the local moonrise time and arrive about fifteen minutes early. A pair of binoculars shows the rim of the disk wobbling in the shimmering air near the horizon.

Season changes the view. For mid-northern latitudes, a winter full moon climbs high overhead and stays up for many hours, while a summer full moon traces a low arc across the southern sky. Around the autumn equinox the full moon rises only a little later on consecutive evenings, giving several bright early evenings in a row, an effect traditionally linked to the Harvest Moon.

If you miss the exact night, the Moon will still look full the evening after. You can follow how the shape changes from night to night on [today's moon phase](/moon-phase-today) page, or plan further ahead with the [moon phase calendar](/moon-calendar).
