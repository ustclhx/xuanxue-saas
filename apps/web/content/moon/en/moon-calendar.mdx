## How to use the moon calendar

Start by picking a month from the list. Each month has its own page with a grid of days, and each day has an icon that shows the Moon’s shape. Screen readers can also read the phase name and estimated illumination. The principal phases, new moon, first quarter, full moon and last quarter, are listed with their times. In the daily grid, a major phase is marked on the date it occurs in your time zone. For example, a full moon just after midnight UTC can fall on the previous evening in the Americas.

The heading on each month page includes the year. The current month and the remaining months show this year. A month that has already ended opens to its next occurrence, in the following year.

All times are converted to the time zone set on your device. The Moon's phase at any instant is the same worldwide, but the local clock and calendar date are not, so a phase that happens late in the evening in one place may fall on the following day somewhere else.

Each month page also explains the traditional name of its full moon, from the Wolf Moon in January to the Cold Moon in December. If you only need the full moons, the [full moon calendar](/full-moon-calendar) collects them for the whole year in a single table.

## Phases through the month

On average, the Moon takes about 29.5 days to complete a cycle of phases, called a synodic month. That takes longer than its roughly 27.3-day orbit around Earth relative to the stars, because Earth moves along its own orbit in the meantime and the Moon needs a little extra time to line up with the Sun again.

The cycle begins at new moon, when the Moon is roughly between Earth and the Sun and hidden in the glare. Over the following days a waxing crescent appears in the evening twilight and grows wider each night. About a week in, the first quarter Moon stands high at dusk, with about half of the disk lit.

The waxing gibbous phase follows, and around two weeks after new moon the full moon rises at sunset and stays up all night. After that the process runs in reverse. The waning gibbous rises later each evening, the last quarter appears around midnight, and the waning crescent becomes a morning object low in the east before dawn.

Because 29.5 days is shorter than most calendar months, a given phase tends to fall earlier in successive months. Occasionally a month holds two full moons or two new moons, and February sometimes has none of one or the other.

## Planning a night of moon watching

The phase you want depends on what you hope to see. For the craters and mountains, the days near first quarter and last quarter are ideal. Sunlight strikes the surface at a low angle along the terminator, the boundary between light and dark, so shadows are long and detail stands out even in small binoculars.

For faint stars, meteor showers and the Milky Way, aim for the nights around new moon. The sky is darkest then, and even the thin crescent that follows sets soon after the Sun. Use the daily phases to choose a date near new moon. If you’re going out later in the cycle, look up local moonset to see how much time you’ll have without moonlight. Cloud cover and nearby lighting matter too, even when the Moon is below the horizon.

For a bright, easy target, the full moon is hard to beat. It rises in the east around sunset with a warm tint near the horizon. The [next full moon](/full-moon) page shows the date and time of the full moon in your time zone.

Weather and season matter too. At mid-northern latitudes the winter full moon climbs high and stays up for long hours, while the summer full moon skims low across the south. Check the forecast a day or two ahead, dress for cooler air after dark, and give your eyes several minutes to adjust before you start looking.
