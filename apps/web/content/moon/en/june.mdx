## Where the name comes from

Strawberry Moon is June's name in the North American almanac list, the set of twelve names that the Maine Farmers' Almanac began printing in the 1930s and The Old Farmer's Almanac helped spread. Almanacs explain it with the brief early-summer window when wild strawberries ripen in fields and along forest edges, a harvest that many communities looked forward to each year.

The name often leads to a misunderstanding similar to April's. A low June full moon can look honey-colored or orange, and people sometimes assume that is where the name came from. It is not: the fruit came first, and any warm tint is simply the atmosphere at work.

European traditions offer the Rose Moon for the same month, matching the season when roses are in full bloom, and some lists include the Hot Moon for the start of summer heat. All three names point to the height of the growing year. Our [full moon calendar](/full-moon-calendar) shows when this year's Strawberry Moon falls in your time zone.

## Viewing tips

The Strawberry Moon is the year's low rider. Because the June Sun climbs so high at midday, the full moon opposite it follows a shallow arc that barely rises above the southern horizon at mid-northern latitudes. Farther north, in places like Scotland or southern Scandinavia, it can stay remarkably close to the treetops all night.

That low path has advantages. The Moon spends its whole night near landmarks, which makes it a favorite for photographers lining it up with a skyline, a bridge or a hilltop. It also shines through a thick layer of air, often taking on a gold or amber tint, especially in humid or hazy weather. Our [next full moon](/full-moon) page gives the moonrise time where you are.

Twilight is long in June, and in the far north the sky may never get fully dark. A full moon that rises shortly after sunset can appear in a sky that is still pale blue, which gives a soft, layered look to photos. Starting a few minutes before moonrise lets you see it emerge from the haze.

Evening crescents are harder to catch than in spring, because the Moon's path now meets the western horizon at a shallow angle and the thin crescent sits low in bright twilight. Waiting a few extra days until the Moon is near first quarter solves that. Follow the progression with [today's moon phase](/moon-phase-today). In the southern hemisphere June is midwinter, and the same full moon soars high overhead.
