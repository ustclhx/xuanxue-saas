## Where the name comes from

Cold Moon is the December label in The Old Farmer’s Almanac, which attributes the name to Mohawk usage. The names in modern almanacs come from different places and traditions, and a name associated with winter can appear at a different point in another lunar calendar.

The meaning is plain. December is when winter cold firmly arrives in much of North America and Europe. Ponds skin over with ice, the ground hardens, and early snow often stays on the ground. Naming the month's full moon after the cold gave a shared, easy reference point for the start of winter. Bright moonlit nights historically made winter travel and evening chores more manageable for rural households.

Long Night Moon refers to the long nights around the northern winter solstice. The seasonal name Moon before Yule appears in Maine Farmers’ Almanac records and belongs to the full moon before the midwinter marker. Its precise date depends on the lunar cycle.

## Viewing tips

At mid-northern latitudes, a full moon near the December solstice usually takes a high arc across the sky. It sits roughly opposite the low winter Sun, so it often rises north of east near sunset and crosses high in the south around midnight. It can be visible for nearly the whole night.

That height has practical benefits. The Moon is clear of trees and buildings for hours, and its light travels through less atmosphere when high, so it often looks bright white rather than orange. On snowy ground the moonlight can be strong enough to cast distinct shadows and let you read the landscape without a flashlight. On nights with thin, high cloud, look for a pale ring around the Moon; it is a halo, made as moonlight is bent by ice crystals in the cloud.

Cold nights call for a little preparation. Let binoculars or a telescope sit outside for a while so the optics adjust to the air temperature, and keep spare batteries warm in an inside pocket.
