## Where the name comes from

The Buck Moon belongs to a list of month names popularized by North American almanacs, including the Maine Farmers' Almanac in the 1930s and The Old Farmer's Almanac. Those lists drew on Native American, Colonial American and European folk names, so each name is best read as a seasonal marker rather than a single origin story.

For July, almanacs point to the buck. Male deer shed their antlers in late winter and begin growing a new set in spring. By midsummer the antlers are growing fast under a fuzzy coat of velvet, and anyone who walked field edges at dusk could see the change. Naming the month's full moon after it tied the sky to an easy, visible sign of the season.

Two other names are often listed. The Thunder Moon reflects how common afternoon and evening storms are in July across much of North America. The Hay Moon points to the work of cutting and drying hay before the late-summer heat. Historically, farm families sometimes kept working into the evening by the light of a bright moon, which helps explain why so many of these names follow the farming year.

## Viewing tips

The July full moon rises in the southeast near sunset and stays fairly low for observers at mid-northern latitudes. Because it sits roughly opposite the Sun, and the summer Sun runs high, the full moon traces a short arc across the southern sky. That low path often gives it a warm yellow or orange tint, since its light passes through more of the atmosphere.

Short summer nights mean the full moon is above the horizon for fewer hours than in winter. Watch it just after it clears the trees or rooftops: the moon illusion makes it look larger there, even though its actual size has not changed. Haze and humidity can soften the view, so a clear evening after a storm front passes is often the best chance.

Early in the month or late in the month, look for a thin crescent low in the west after sunset; the faint glow on its dark side is earthshine, sunlight reflected off Earth.

Readers south of the equator see July as midwinter. For them the full moon climbs high and stays up for a long night, and the lit side of each phase appears flipped compared with the northern view.
