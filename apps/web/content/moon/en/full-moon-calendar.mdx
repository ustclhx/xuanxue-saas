## How to read the table

Each row of the table describes one full moon. The first column gives the date, the second the time of the exact full moon, and the third its traditional name. A small marker appears when that full moon counts as a supermoon or as a blue moon.

When the page first loads, dates and times are shown in UTC, the shared reference time used by astronomers. A moment later the table switches to the time zone of your device, so the date you see is the one that applies where you are. If you are traveling, the values follow whatever time zone your phone or computer is set to.

Times are rounded to the nearest minute. The underlying calculation follows standard astronomical algorithms for the positions of the Sun and Moon, and is accurate well within that rounding for practical observing.

Names follow the calendar month in which the full moon falls in your time zone. A full moon that occurs in the first hours of a month takes that month's name even if it was still the previous month in UTC. The one exception to the monthly pattern is the Harvest Moon, explained below.

## Where the traditional names come from

The list most English speakers know, from the Wolf Moon in January to the Cold Moon in December, was popularized by North American almanacs, notably the Maine Farmers' Almanac in the 1930s and later The Old Farmer's Almanac. The almanac writers drew on a mix of Native American, Colonial American and European folk names, and they assigned one name to each calendar month for convenience.

Many of the names describe the season in the northern temperate zone. The Snow Moon falls in the snowiest stretch of winter, the Strawberry Moon matches the short wild strawberry season in early summer, and the Beaver Moon is often linked to the time when beavers retreat to their lodges and trappers set their lines. The Pink Moon refers to wild ground phlox, sometimes called moss pink, that blooms in early spring, not to any change in the Moon's color.

Two names follow a rule instead of a month. The Harvest Moon is the full moon closest to the September equinox, so in some years it lands in October and replaces the Corn Moon. The Hunter's Moon is the full moon after the Harvest Moon. Historically, farmers and hunters used the bright early-evening moonlight around these dates to keep working after sunset.

Every name has alternatives. The Wolf Moon is also called the Old Moon or Ice Moon, the Cold Moon is known as the Long Night Moon, and the Buck Moon has been called the Thunder Moon or Hay Moon. Treat the names as cultural labels rather than descriptions of anything the Moon does.

## Two full moons in one month

The cycle of phases, the synodic month, averages about 29.5 days. Every calendar month except February is longer than that, so if a full moon lands on the first or second day of a month, there is room for another before the month ends.

When that happens, the second full moon of the month is called a blue moon under the monthly definition this table uses. It happens roughly once every two to three years. February can never have two full moons, and in some years it has none at all.

An older seasonal definition also exists. It counts full moons within each astronomical season and calls the third of four a blue moon. Both meanings are in use today, and neither describes the Moon's actual color.
