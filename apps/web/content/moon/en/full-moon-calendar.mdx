## How to read the table

Each row of the table describes one full moon. The month comes first, followed by the date and time of the full moon and its traditional name. A marker appears beside the name for a supermoon or blue moon. The last column lists any new moon dates for that month.

When the page first loads, dates and times are shown in UTC, the shared reference time used by astronomers. A moment later the table switches to the time zone of your device, so the date you see is the one that applies where you are. If you are traveling, the values follow whatever time zone your phone or computer is set to.

Times are shown to the minute. The calculation uses the positions of the Sun and Moon to find the instant of full moon. Local moonrise depends on where you are, so look up moonrise for your location if you plan to watch the Moon near the horizon.

Names follow the calendar month in which the full moon falls in your time zone. A full moon that occurs in the first hours of a month takes that month's name even if it was still the previous month in UTC. Two full moons in the same month share that month’s label. The Harvest Moon and the seasonal Hunter’s Moon follow the rules below, while the table uses the same set of month names each year.

## Where the traditional names come from

North American almanacs publish the English names used here, from January’s Wolf Moon to December’s Cold Moon. Their lists bring together names from different sources, including Indigenous lunar calendars as well as colonial American and European usage. The same name can refer to a different part of the year in another tradition. The Maine Farmers’ Almanac organized names around the seasons, and The Old Farmer’s Almanac also publishes a month-by-month list.

Many of the names describe the season in the northern temperate zone. The Snow Moon falls in the snowiest stretch of winter, the Strawberry Moon matches the short wild strawberry season in early summer, and the Beaver Moon is often linked to the time when beavers retreat to their lodges and trappers set their lines. The Pink Moon refers to wild ground phlox, sometimes called moss pink, that blooms in early spring, not to any change in the Moon's color.

Two names follow a rule instead of a month. The Harvest Moon is the full moon closest to the September equinox, so in some years it falls in October. September still carries the Corn Moon label in this table. The Hunter's Moon is the full moon after the Harvest Moon. Historically, farmers and hunters used the bright early-evening moonlight around these dates to keep working after sunset.

Some English lists also give Old Moon or Ice Moon for January and Hay Moon for July. The Old Farmer’s Almanac records Long Night Moon as a Mohican name and Thunder Moon as a Western Abenaki name. The month pages explain where these names come from and what they refer to.

## Two full moons in one month

The cycle of phases, the synodic month, averages about 29.5 days. Every calendar month except February is longer than that, so two full moons can fit in a long month when the first falls early enough. The exact spacing also varies slightly from one lunar cycle to the next.

When that happens, the table marks the second full moon in the same local calendar month as a blue moon. It happens roughly once every two to three years. February can never have two full moons, and in some years it has none at all.

The seasonal definition counts the third of four full moons between one equinox or solstice and the next. This meaning has roots in the Maine Farmers’ Almanac, which used its own rules for seasonal boundaries. Both meanings refer to timing, regardless of the Moon’s color.
