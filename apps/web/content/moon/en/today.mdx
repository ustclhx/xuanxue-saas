## The eight phases

**New Moon.** The Moon sits roughly between Earth and the Sun, so its lit side faces away from us. It rises and sets with the Sun and is lost in daylight glare, which makes these the darkest nights of the cycle for stargazing.

**Waxing Crescent.** A thin sliver appears low in the west shortly after sunset and follows the Sun down within a few hours. Look closely and you may see earthshine, a faint glow on the unlit part caused by sunlight reflected off Earth.

**First Quarter.** Half of the visible disk is lit. The Moon rises around midday, stands high in the south at dusk for northern observers and sets near midnight. The shadow line, called the terminator, shows crater rims in sharp relief.

**Waxing Gibbous.** More than half is lit and the shape bulges toward fullness. It rises in the afternoon and dominates the evening sky, staying up well past midnight.

**Full Moon.** The Moon lies on the far side of Earth from the Sun, so the whole near side is lit. It rises in the east near sunset, crosses the sky overnight and sets around sunrise. You can check the timing of the [full moon tonight](/full-moon) before planning an evening out.

**Waning Gibbous.** The lit portion begins to shrink from the opposite edge. The Moon now rises an hour or so later each evening and is often easiest to spot in the western sky after dawn.

**Last Quarter.** The other half of the disk is lit. It rises around midnight and is high in the south at sunrise, a familiar sight for early commuters on a clear morning.

**Waning Crescent.** A narrowing sliver rises in the east before dawn and fades into the brightening sky. Earthshine returns on the dark side, and within a few days the cycle closes with another new moon.

## How the phase is calculated

The phase depends on the angle between the Sun and the Moon as seen from Earth. When that angle, called the elongation, is close to zero we have a new moon; near 90 degrees we see a quarter; near 180 degrees the Moon is full. Everything shown on this page follows from computing the positions of the Sun and Moon for the current moment.

From that angle we derive the illumination fraction, the share of the visible disk that is lit by sunlight. It climbs from zero at new moon to one hundred percent at full moon and then falls again. Because the Moon's orbit is elliptical and tilted by about five degrees, the percentage does not change at a perfectly even rate.

Moon age is the number of days since the most recent new moon. A full cycle of phases, the synodic month, averages about 29.5 days, so an age of around seven days points to first quarter and an age near fifteen points to full moon.

At a single instant the phase is the same for everyone on the planet. The calendar date of that instant, however, depends on your time zone. A full moon that occurs shortly before midnight in London happens the following morning in Tokyo by local reckoning. This page reads your device's time zone and converts every phase time accordingly. ## Reading the Moon in the sky

A quick rule helps in the northern hemisphere: if the right side is lit, the Moon is waxing and will grow over the coming nights; if the left side is lit, it is waning. South of the equator the pattern is reversed, and near the equator the crescent often appears to lie on its back like a bowl.

The time of night also tells you a lot. A Moon visible in the early evening sky is usually waxing, while one hanging in the west after sunrise is waning. The Moon rises roughly fifty minutes later each day on average, although the actual delay varies with season and latitude.

Binoculars reveal more than you might expect. Along the terminator, low sunlight casts long shadows from mountains and crater walls, so the days around the quarters show the most surface detail. The full moon looks flat by comparison, but it is the best time to trace bright ray systems spreading from young craters.
