## The eight phases

**New Moon.** The Moon sits roughly between Earth and the Sun, so its lit side faces away from us. It rises and sets with the Sun and is lost in daylight glare, which makes these the darkest nights of the cycle for stargazing.

**Waxing Crescent.** A thin sliver appears low in the west shortly after sunset and follows the Sun down within a few hours. Look closely and you may see earthshine, a faint glow on the unlit part caused by sunlight reflected off Earth.

**First Quarter.** About half of the visible disk is lit. The Moon rises around midday, stands high in the south at dusk for northern observers and sets near midnight. The shadow line, called the terminator, shows crater rims in sharp relief.

**Waxing Gibbous.** More than half is lit and the shape bulges toward fullness. It rises in the afternoon and dominates the evening sky, staying up well past midnight.

**Full Moon.** The Moon lies on the far side of Earth from the Sun, so almost all of the visible disk is lit outside a lunar eclipse. It rises in the east near sunset, crosses the sky overnight and sets around sunrise. You can check the timing of the [full moon tonight](/full-moon) before planning an evening out.

**Waning Gibbous.** The lit portion begins to shrink from the opposite edge. The Moon generally rises later each evening and is often easiest to spot in the western sky after dawn.

**Last Quarter.** The Moon is waning, and about half of the visible disk is lit. It rises around midnight and is high in the south at sunrise, a familiar sight for early commuters on a clear morning.

**Waning Crescent.** A narrowing sliver rises in the east before dawn and fades into the brightening sky. Earthshine returns on the dark side, and within a few days the cycle closes with another new moon.

## How the phase is calculated

The four major phases are defined by a difference in ecliptic longitude, measured along the Sun’s apparent path as seen from Earth’s center. The Moon is 0 degrees ahead of the Sun at new moon, 90 at first quarter, 180 at full moon and 270 at last quarter. The phase times on this page come from those four alignments.

Illumination is the share of the visible disk lit directly by the Sun in the calculation. It is near zero at new moon, about 50 percent at the quarters and close to 100 percent at full moon. The calendar uses a sample for each UTC date, taken at noon UTC or at the time of a major phase if one occurs on that UTC date.

Moon age is the number of days since the most recent new moon. A full cycle of phases, the synodic month, averages about 29.5 days, so an age of around seven days points to first quarter and an age near fifteen points to full moon.

The calculated instant of a major phase is the same everywhere. The calendar date of that instant, however, depends on your time zone. A full moon that occurs shortly before midnight in London happens the following morning in Tokyo by local reckoning. This page reads your device's time zone and converts every phase time accordingly. For the year ahead, the [full moon calendar](/full-moon-calendar) lists each date in the same way.

## Reading the Moon in the sky

At mid-northern latitudes, a waxing crescent often looks brighter on the right, and a waning crescent on the left. At mid-southern latitudes, the pattern often looks reversed. Near the equator you may see a crescent that looks like a bowl. For a more reliable clue, look at whether the bright portion gets wider or narrower over several days.

The time of night also tells you a lot. A Moon visible in the early evening sky is usually waxing, while one hanging in the west after sunrise is waning. The Moon rises roughly fifty minutes later each day on average, although the actual delay varies with season and latitude.

Binoculars reveal more than you might expect. Along the terminator, low sunlight casts long shadows from mountains and crater walls, so the days around the quarters are good for observing surface relief. The full moon looks flat by comparison, but it is a good time to trace bright rays spreading from some craters.

If you want to follow the whole month day by day, the [moon calendar](/moon-calendar) shows each phase laid out by date, so you can see in advance which evenings will be dark and which will be bright.
