## The old custom of matching charts before a wedding

For many generations in China, an engagement often began with an exchange of papers. Each family wrote out the year, month, day and hour of birth for their son or daughter, and the two sets of details were taken to a matchmaker or a local practitioner. That person laid out both BaZi charts and compared them according to traditional rules. Similar customs grew up in Korea and Japan, each with its own local name and emphasis.

The matching was never the only factor. Families weighed reputation, distance, trade and, increasingly over time, what the couple themselves wanted. The chart comparison worked more like a structured conversation starter than a final ruling, and practitioners differed widely in what they emphasized. Some looked mostly at zodiac animals, which is the year branch. More careful readers focused on the Day Pillar, because it represents the individual rather than their birth year.

BaZi marriage compatibility on this page follows that more personal line. It compares the two Day Masters, the two Day Branches and each partner's favorable elements, and turns the result into a single reference score.

## Why the Day Branch carries the spouse position

In a BaZi chart, each pillar is linked to an area of life. The year pillar is associated with ancestry and the wider family, the month pillar with upbringing and work, and the hour pillar with later life. The Day Pillar is the self. Its stem, the Day Master, is the person; its branch, sitting directly beneath, is traditionally the spouse palace.

That is why marriage matching gives so much weight to how two Day Branches meet. A six harmony between them, such as Chen (辰) and You (酉), was read as partners who share private space easily. Two branches from the same three-harmony set, such as Yin (寅) and Xu (戌) from the fire group, suggested a shared direction. A clash, such as Si (巳) against Hai (亥), was seen as two people who move through the day differently, and a punishment pointed to friction in small things.

The calculator scores these relationships and keeps them visible in the breakdown, so you can see which ones applied to your pair rather than only a total.

## Running a household on two rhythms

Most of married life is ordinary: who cooks, who handles paperwork, how weekends go, how much quiet each person needs. The traditional readings translate surprisingly well into these questions if you take them as descriptions of rhythm rather than fate.

If your result shows a clash, look at the daily schedule first. One of you may wake early and wind down early while the other hits their stride at night. Neither pattern is a problem on its own. It becomes one only when it goes unspoken. A short weekly conversation about the week ahead, with each partner naming one thing they need, can absorb a lot of the friction the old texts describe.

If the element complement leans heavily one way, think about support in the same practical terms. Perhaps one partner is the steady planner and the other brings warmth and momentum. Saying out loud what each person contributes keeps the balance from being taken for granted.

## What the score leaves out

A marriage involves trust, shared values, patience, money habits, families of origin and many years of choices. None of these appear in two dates of birth, and none of them are measured here. The score starts at 60 and moves only according to which classical rules match, so two people with a strong, caring marriage can easily see a modest number.

This comparison is calculated with traditional methods and is offered for reference and self-reflection only; it does not predict the future of any marriage. If you are deciding something important about your relationship, the most reliable information comes from each other, and where needed from a trusted counselor.
