## The old custom of matching charts before a wedding

In some Chinese wedding traditions, a matchmaker carried birth information between two families during betrothal. The date and hour of each person’s birth supplied the basis for their BaZi, the eight characters used in the comparison. A practitioner used those details to read the charts. Records of betrothal customs place the exchange alongside other steps, such as introductions and negotiations between families.

The order and importance of those steps varied by place. Historical accounts describe relatives and matchmakers handling much of the exchange. On this page each partner can look directly at the same breakdown, point to the item that caught their attention, and decide for themselves whether it brings any aspect of their daily life to mind.

This BaZi marriage compatibility calculator uses a fixed set of rules. It compares the two Day Masters, the two Day Branches, and each partner’s favorable elements. The point breakdown sits alongside the reference score so you can see how it was calculated.

## Why the Day Branch carries the spouse position

In a BaZi chart, each pillar is linked to an area of life. The year pillar is associated with ancestry and the wider family, the month pillar with upbringing and work, and the hour pillar with later life. Within that framework, the Day Master is the stem that represents the person. The branch directly below it is traditionally called the spouse palace.

Here the two Day Branches are checked against four traditional rules. A six-harmony pair such as Chen (辰) and You (酉) can serve as an image of sharing space. Yin (寅) and Xu (戌), from the three-harmony fire group, suggest the image of a shared direction. A clash such as Si (巳) and Hai (亥) provides an image of contrast. The rule translated as punishment can prompt you to think about differences over everyday details.

The calculator shows every applicable pairing separately. The same score can come from different combinations of rules, so the lines under the total are where you can see which rules applied to your charts.

## Running a household on two rhythms

Most of married life is ordinary. Someone cooks, someone handles paperwork, and both people need to fit errands and rest into a weekend. Pick one of those familiar scenes when you look through the breakdown.

If a clash makes you think of your daily schedule, start there. One of you may wind down early while the other hits their stride at night. Try talking about the week ahead. Each of you can name one thing you need and one commitment you already have. That gives you concrete information to use when you make plans.

The two element lines can bring support to mind. Ask your partner which part of the week felt crowded, or name one task they took care of that you appreciated. For one couple it might be sorting a repair. For another it might be leaving the kitchen quiet while the other person finishes work.

If you’re getting ready to share a home, talk about a routine each of you brings from where you live now. You may discover that the same words, like “a quiet Sunday,” describe different things to each of you. Ask what each person pictures.

## What the score leaves out

A marriage involves trust, shared values, family commitments, and years of decisions together. Those are all parts of your lived history. The score starts at 60 and changes according to the traditional pairings and the two favorable-element calculations. It stays the same when you select a different relationship scenario for the same charts.

This comparison uses traditional methods and is offered for reference and self-reflection only. It does not predict the future of a marriage. The observations you can make together come from what each of you says and does in the home you share.
