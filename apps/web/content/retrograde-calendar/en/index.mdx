## What retrograde means

A planet is retrograde when, seen from Earth, it appears to drift backward against the stars for a few weeks or months. Most of the time the planets creep eastward through the zodiac, sign after sign. During a retrograde the direction flips and the planet slides westward, retracing a stretch of sky it has just crossed, before it turns again and resumes its usual course.

Nothing about the planet’s orbit changes. Every planet keeps circling the Sun in the same direction. The reversal is a matter of viewpoint: Earth and the other planet travel at different speeds on orbits of different sizes, and as one overtakes the other, the line of sight between them swings backward for a while. For Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and the planets beyond, that happens around opposition, when Earth passes between the Sun and the planet on the inside track. Mercury and Venus orbit closer to the Sun than Earth does, so their retrogrades come around inferior conjunction, when they pass between Earth and the Sun.

The two turning points are called stations. Near a station the planet barely moves along the zodiac and seems to pause before reversing. Each date in the tables marks one station: the day a planet turns retrograde, and the day it turns direct again. The Moon never goes retrograde; what changes from night to night is its phase, which you can follow on [today's moon phase](/moon-phase-today) page.

## How often each planet turns

Mercury retrogrades most often. Three or four of its retrogrades begin in each calendar year, each lasting about three weeks, and during each one it slips back between about 8° and 16° of the zodiac. Venus is the least frequent: roughly once every 19 months, for about six weeks. Mars turns about every two years and two months and stays retrograde for 60 to 81 days, which is why Mars and Venus each skip some calendar years entirely.

The outer planets move slowly, so Earth laps them about once a year. Jupiter is retrograde for about four months at a time, Saturn for about four and a half, and Uranus, Neptune and Pluto for roughly five months each. Over a year that adds up: Mercury is retrograde on about 19% of days and Venus on about 7%, while the planets from Jupiter outward are retrograde on between about 30% and 44% of days. On most days of the year at least one planet is retrograde, so a retrograde on the calendar is ordinary.

The Signs column names the [zodiac signs](/zodiac-signs) the planet passes through while it is retrograde, starting from the sign where it turns. Because Uranus, Neptune and Pluto back up only a few degrees, they usually stay in a single sign, while Mercury often crosses back into the previous one.

## Retrograde in astrological tradition

Astrologers traditionally read a retrograde as a time to look back rather than push ahead: to review, revise, repair and finish what is already underway. Each planet’s retrograde is read through the part of life tradition gives that planet. Mercury is linked with thinking and speech, Venus with affection and taste, Mars with drive, and Saturn with structure and time. Many astrologers also watch the shadow, the weeks when a planet first crosses the stretch of zodiac it will later retrace, and the weeks after it turns direct while it covers that ground again.

These associations are a traditional reading offered for reflection. A retrograde does not cause events, and this calendar is not a forecast. It shows only when a planet’s apparent motion reverses, so you can see the timing and decide for yourself whether the traditional themes are worth a moment of thought.

## How the dates are calculated

For each planet from Mercury to Pluto we compute its apparent geocentric longitude: its position along the zodiac as seen from Earth, corrected for the time its light takes to reach us. A station is the moment the daily change in that longitude crosses zero. The search steps through the calendar one to four days at a time and then narrows each crossing down to the second. The results are worked out ahead of time for this year and next, so the page does not compute planet positions when it opens.

We compared every station from 2025 through 2028 with Swiss Ephemeris, a reference used by professional astrology software. All of them fall on the same UTC date. For Mercury, Venus and Mars the times agree to within about seven minutes. Near a station the slow outer planets barely move, so small differences between ephemerides shift the computed moment by more: for Neptune and Pluto the gap reaches about 41 minutes. That is why the tables give dates rather than times of day.
