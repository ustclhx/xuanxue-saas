## What retrograde means

A planet is retrograde when, seen from Earth, it appears to drift backward against the stars for a few weeks or months. Most of the time the planets creep eastward through the zodiac, sign after sign. During a retrograde the direction flips and the planet slides westward, retracing a stretch of sky it has just crossed, before it turns again and resumes its usual course.

The planet keeps circling the Sun in the same direction. The reversal we see comes from a changing line of sight as Earth and the planet move along their orbits at different speeds. For bodies that orbit beyond Earth, from Mars through Pluto, that happens around opposition, when Earth passes roughly between the Sun and the other body. Mercury and Venus orbit closer to the Sun than Earth does, so their retrogrades come around inferior conjunction, when they pass roughly between Earth and the Sun.

The two turning points are called stations. Near a station the planet barely moves along the zodiac and seems to pause before reversing. The tables show the date of each station, first when the planet starts moving westward (turns retrograde) and then when it resumes moving eastward (turns direct). The Moon’s eastward motion changes how much of its sunlit side we see. You can see that change by following [today's moon phase](/moon-phase-today).

## How often each planet turns

Mercury retrogrades most often. Three or four of its retrogrades begin in each calendar year. Each lasts about three weeks, during which Mercury moves westward between about 8° and 16° of the zodiac. Venus goes retrograde roughly once every 19 months, for about six weeks. Mars turns about every two years and two months and stays retrograde for 60 to 81 days. That leaves some calendar years with no Mars retrograde and some with no Venus retrograde.

Jupiter and the bodies farther out move slowly enough that Earth laps them about once a year. Jupiter is retrograde for about four months at a time, Saturn for about four and a half, and Uranus, Neptune and Pluto for roughly five months each. In our long-term calculations, Mercury is retrograde on about 19% of days and Venus on about 7%. For Jupiter through Pluto, the share ranges between about 30% and 44% of days. On most days of the year at least one planet is retrograde.

The Signs column names the [zodiac signs](/zodiac-signs) the planet passes through while it is retrograde, starting from the sign where it turns. Because Uranus, Neptune and Pluto back up only a few degrees, they usually stay in a single sign, while Mercury often crosses back into the previous one.

## Retrograde in astrological tradition

In Western astrology, some astrologers use retrogrades as a prompt to revisit familiar themes. They associate Mercury with thinking and speech, Venus with affection and aesthetic taste, Mars with drive, and Saturn with structure and time.

Some astrologers also track the direct passes on either side of a retrograde. The pre-retrograde shadow begins when the planet first reaches the zodiac degree where it will later turn direct. It ends at the retrograde station. The post-retrograde shadow begins when the planet turns direct and ends when it reaches the degree where it went retrograde. The calendar marks the stations that bound the retrograde itself.

## How the dates are calculated

We calculate the apparent position of each body from Mercury to Pluto along the zodiac as seen from Earth. This position is called apparent geocentric longitude and accounts for effects such as the time light takes to reach us. A station occurs when the rate of change in longitude passes through zero. We check the rate at intervals of one to four days, depending on the planet, then narrow the crossing to a one-second interval in the model. We calculate the dates ahead of time and your browser displays them in your time zone.

The computed times are estimates. We checked all 71 stations from 2025 through 2028 against Swiss Ephemeris. Each one fell on the same UTC date in both calculations. The tests allow differences of up to 8 minutes for Mercury, Venus and Mars, 15 minutes for Jupiter and Saturn, 20 minutes for Uranus, and 45 minutes for Neptune and Pluto. The tables show calendar dates. Near midnight, even a small difference in the estimated time can change the date in your time zone.
