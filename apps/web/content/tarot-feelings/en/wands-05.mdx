## What the Five of Wands shows about how someone feels

Five young men in mismatched tunics raise their staffs at one another, and the staffs cross in a tangle above their heads. Look closely and no one is hurt; no one is even clearly winning. It looks more like a scuffle in a schoolyard than a battle.

Read as feelings, that tangle suggests agitation. Someone feels stirred up, competitive and unsure where they stand. There may be attraction in the mix, but it is tangled with pride, defensiveness and the need to be heard first.

## Upright: sparks that look like sparring

Upright, the keywords are competition and friction. In a romantic context this can look like constant teasing that sometimes stings, debates that run late, or a pattern of one-upping each other’s stories. Some couples enjoy that friction, and some flirtations run on it.

The limit is that friction wears people out. The card leans no as a straight answer because, at the moment, the energy is scattered rather than aligned. Attraction can coexist with this, but it rarely settles until someone stops swinging.

## Reversed: putting the staffs down

Reversed, avoided conflict and compromise appear. The fight may calm because both people agreed to meet halfway, which is genuinely healthy. It may also calm because someone decided disagreement is not worth the trouble and stopped saying what they feel.

Notice which version you are in. Real compromise leaves both people lighter. Swallowed conflict tends to come back as sarcasm or distance, often weeks later and about something that looks unrelated.

## Five of Wands as feelings from an ex

From an ex, the card can describe unfinished arguments. They may still feel the need to be right about the breakup, to explain their side, or to compete over who is doing better afterward. Contact can carry an edge, even when it looks friendly.

Whether that energy means they still care stays outside what the image can show. Wanting to win an argument with you is not the same as wanting you back. If a conversation becomes a rematch, you are free to step out of it.

## Among friends

This card is at home in a friend group. Picture two friends who both like the same person, or a group where your new relationship has shifted the balance and people feel competitive for your time. It can also describe a friendship with a crackle of rivalry that both of you secretly enjoy.

Friction among friends is often about belonging. Someone may simply be afraid of being replaced. A friend who suddenly criticizes your partner, or who competes for your weekends, might be expressing that fear in the only language they have at the moment.

None of this means the friendship is failing. Groups reshuffle whenever someone falls in love, changes jobs or moves. Naming the shift out loud, with some humor if you can manage it, usually helps more than pretending nothing changed.

## Finding the one real disagreement

Before you do anything, give yourself a day to cool off after any heated exchange, because staffs swung in a hurry rarely land where you meant them to. Under most scuffles there is one actual issue. This week, try to name it in a single sentence, then share it calmly: “I think we keep arguing because I feel second to your plans.” If the other person can respond to the real point, the noise often drops quickly.
